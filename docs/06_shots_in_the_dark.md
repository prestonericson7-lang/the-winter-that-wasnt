# 6. Shots in the dark

Everything here is **SPECULATION**: links across gaps the released record does not fill. Each says what supports it, what cuts against it, and
the test that would settle it.

## 1. The quiet after 1972

**The link.** The target indexing device, the last machine of the automatic target recognition line, is reviewed by COMIREX in September and
December 1972 and its maintenance manuals are under way; after that the released record says nothing about it, or about automatic target
recognition, until 1984 (MEASURED). Either NPIC fielded it and moved on, ended the work, renamed it again, or the later records were never
released into CREST.
**Supports it.** NPIC's five-year plan ran to FY-76 with pattern recognition in it; the 1974 subcommittee still wanted automated pattern
recognition; the 1984 steering group still listed it.
**Cuts against it.** The 1974 and 1984 papers describe the problem as unsolved, which fits a program that ended or shrank, not one that went black.
**The test.** A FOIA request to the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency for COMIREX-D-30.5/1 (December 8, 1972) and for NPIC records on the
"Target Indexing Device," 1972 to 1980.
*Beside this, the morning of November 22:* the device was delivered to NPIC in December 1970 and installed in Room 2N617, and the machine reading of imagery returns to the released record in 1982 under a new name, "image understanding," bought from Hughes, with a $1.7 million testbed requested in January 1983 ([docs/01](01_the_cia_line.md)). The quiet was a change of name, at least in part.

## 2. The company that taught itself

**The link.** The June 20, 1969 trip report's sanitized company had a coherent-optical diffraction system "designed and developed ... starting in
1961," built "without external funding," with "an adaptive learning network dependent, in part, on human intuition." ESTIMATE, strong: it was
General Motors' Defense Research Laboratory in Santa Barbara. An NPIC consultant named that laboratory, unsanitized, under "Automatic Target
Recognition" in October 1965 for laser diffraction "signatures of various terrain features and culture," and its Lendaris and Stanley published
the method, with "algorithm development (using an interactive computer-graphic based facility)," in February 1970.
**Supports it.** The same technique, the same interactive way of finding the decision rules, the same sorting of natural from man-made,
California, and a four-year acquaintance; and in 1965 the reviewers sanitized GM's name in one copy of the same paragraph and missed it in
another, so GM was a name these records hide; NPIC chose diffraction pattern sampling for its device in September 1969.
**Cuts against it.** The 1969 record itself is sanitized, and "starting in 1961" is earlier than any GM paper I found.
**The test.** The unsanitized trip report (NPIC/TSSG/QED-1676-69) by FOIA; who built the target indexing device (the December 1972 COMIREX
paper, COMIREX-D-30.5/1); the 1970 paper's acknowledgments.
*Beside this, the morning of November 22:* the device itself was built by Litton Systems of Van Nuys (ESTIMATE, strong: three records name Litton; [docs/02](02_the_contractor.md#a-third-name-the-devices-maker)). The May 1969 company, self-funded since 1961, is still most likely GM, but Litton is now a second name the record puts beside the technique; the unsanitized trip report would settle it.

## 3. The machine that would "memorize ... recognize"

**The link.** The FY-72 plan (conference of February 1971) proposed an "Automated Target Rescan Device," a system that "can memorize the
information at a target site and then recognize this and compare and identify it on other imagery." That is a learning machine described
without the words.
**Supports it.** NPIC had run CONFLEX in 1964 to 1966 and paid for "faster machine learning algorithms" in 1969.
**Cuts against it.** Nothing in the released record shows it was built.
**The test.** The FY-72 R&D budget and contract records; FOIA for "Automated Target Rescan Device."

## 4. Scope's radar years

**The link.** The CIA's 1971 plans asked whether target recognition could work on radar imagery (a "SLAR/ATR Feasibility Study"); Scope Inc.'s
Malcolm Uffelman is named on a radar target pattern recognizer patent around 1980 (UNVERIFIED), and he is still in the intelligence
contractors' association's lists in 1984. Speculation: Scope carried the learning-machine line from CONFLEX (1962) into radar target
recognition through the 1970s.
**Supports it.** The same engineer, the same company, the same problem, eighteen years apart.
**Cuts against it.** A patent is public; nothing links the patent to the CIA.
**The test.** The patent's text and its government-interest clause, if any; Scope Inc.'s defense reports from 1968 to 1980.

## 5. The NSA's speech recognizers

**The link.** The NSA's journal has speaker identification by machine (1969), Markov models for phonetic text (1973) and keyword spotting in
intercepted voice (1976 and 1977), and the hidden Markov training method came from its Princeton contractor in 1970. Speculation: by the late
1970s the NSA was spotting keywords in intercepted voice with trained hidden Markov models, about when the open world built its first ones.
**Cuts against it.** The other titles of those years say "statistical pattern recognition" and "nearest neighbor rule," tools that need no
Markov models at all.
**The test.** The articles themselves; several *NSA Technical Journal* issues are declassified and online at nsa.gov.

## 6. The electric fish

**The link.** In 1973 and 1974 the CIA paid a contractor (name deleted) to study how weakly electric fish recognize patterns, to build
"physical analogs of the electroreceptors" for "underwater object detection." Speculation: a quiet bionics line, learning from living pattern
recognizers, ran through the winter beside the target recognition work (a 1971 record on echolocation in bats sits in the same corner).
**The test.** The full contract file and the final report (documents 00173502, 00173966, 00173971 in the reading room).

## 7. A capability held elsewhere

**The link.** The CIA's records are one agency's. The Army (the Tactical Target Recognizer), GIMRADA (the Natural Image Computer), Rome Air
Development Center (an Automatic Target Recognition Device) and Fort Monmouth (Stanford's MINOS II) all had learning or recognition machines in
the 1960s, and by 1984 a signals-intelligence contractor had learning classifiers "improving performance during system operation."
Speculation: one of them kept a learning machine in operational use through the 1970s.
**Cuts against it.** The 1984 steering group, which spoke for the whole intelligence community, said pattern recognition on imagery was "NOT
USED TODAY."
**The test.** Defense reports for each program by name, 1966 to 1985; ESL's signal classification contracts.
